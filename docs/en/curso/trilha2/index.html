🧭 Autonomous Robot

The Track 1 rover gets phone control, a sense of position, and a real control brain. You’ll set up Wi-Fi and BLE, measure rotation and distance with an IMU and encoders, master PID, run several tasks at once, log everything in graphs, and finish with a PID line follower that you tune by looking at the data.

🏁 What you’ll build in this track

The same rover from Track 1, now with wheel encoders, an IMU, and a line sensor bar underneath. Along the way, you’ll build a joystick controller in your phone’s browser, a tilt meter, the robot’s position in x, y, and θ, two wheels that move at the same speed, a data logger on a microSD card, and a live dashboard. At the end, the robot follows a black line through sharp turns and intersections, racing against the clock.

  • ✓PID you can test on a simulated motor
  • ✓Safe stop when the signal drops
  • ✓A direct foundation for micro-ROS in Track 4

💡 Before you start

You need the rover from 1.6 up and running and know how to use the Serial Monitor. PID (2.3) and non-blocking code (2.4) run in Wokwi; the IMU and encoders require the kit components listed below.

📱 Phone control: Wi-Fi and BLE

Wi-Fi in station and access point modes, a page with buttons, low-latency WebSocket, BLE with a generic app, a command watchdog, and a browser joystick for driving the rover.

What it is:

The two ways the ESP32 can use Wi-Fi: join your home network or create its own, with a sketch that tries one and falls back to the other.

Why learn this:

All wireless control and telemetry start here; choosing the right mode prevents a robot that won’t connect at a science fair.

Key concepts:

STA · AP · IP address · 2.4 GHz.

What it is:

The 3.x WebServer library serving a page with five buttons that send commands to the robot.

Why learn this:

Any phone becomes a controller, with no app to install, and the same server can later adjust parameters.

Key concepts:

Route · GET request · handleClient · fetch.

What it is:

A channel that stays open between the browser and the ESP32, carrying short commands in both directions.

Why learn this:

A joystick and continuous telemetry need low latency; HTTP opens and closes a connection for every command.

Key concepts:

Latency · open channel · two-way · WebSockets library.

What it is:

Low-energy Bluetooth with the BLE library built into the esp32 package: one service, one characteristic, and a generic app.

Why learn this:

Works without any network and uses the same pattern as wireless controllers and sensors.

Key concepts:

Service · characteristic · UUID · advertising.

What it is:

Password on the robot’s network and a command watchdog: if no new message arrives for 300 ms, the robot stops on its own.

Why learn this:

The signal drops; a robot that keeps going with its last “forward” command will crash. Fail-safe behavior is a rule for every real robot.

Key concepts:

Watchdog · timeout · fail-safe · maximum speed on the robot.

What it is:

Complete sketch: access point, joystick page, WebSocket, differential mixing, and watchdog, driving the real rover.

Why learn this:

Every autonomous robot needs this manual mode for testing and recovery.

Key concepts:

Differential mixing · pointer events · raw string · 10 sends per second.

🧭 IMU and encoders: know where you are

Accelerometer and gyroscope, the MPU-6050 read directly through its registers, the complementary filter, quadrature encoders, and odometry in x, y, and θ, including the errors that accumulate.

What it is:

The chip that senses gravity and rotation on three axes, and the strength and weakness of each sensor.

Why learn this:

Turning exactly 90°, sensing a ramp, and detecting a fall all depend on it.

Key concepts:

g · °/s · integration · drift · MPU-6050 vs. ICM-20948.

What it is:

Four wires and a sketch that wakes the sensor and reads 14 bytes of acceleration and gyroscope data, with no extra library.

Why learn this:

The same approach works for almost any I2C sensor you use later.

Key concepts:

Register · address 0x68 · burst read · unit conversion.

What it is:

Combine the gyroscope (fast, drifts) and accelerometer (slow, noisy) to get a stable tilt reading, with three curves in the Serial Plotter.

Why learn this:

This is the simplest form of sensor fusion and a first step toward the Kalman filter.

Key concepts:

atan2 · gyroscope offset · α = 0,98 · fixed dt.

What it is:

A disk or magnet that generates pulses; two quadrature channels give you direction; an interrupt counts every pulse.

Why learn this:

The wheel’s actual speed is the PID input and the basis for odometry.

Key concepts:

Pulses per revolution · quadrature · ISR with IRAM_ATTR · volatile.

What it is:

Five lines of calculations add up how far each wheel has traveled and turn that into position and direction.

Why learn this:

Returning to base and moving to a point require knowing where you are; this is what micro-ROS publishes in Track 4.

Key concepts:

Pose · meters per pulse · distance between wheels · radians.

What it is:

Why the estimated position drifts from the real one, and a four-step calibration that reduces the error.

Why learn this:

Shows when odometry is enough and when the robot needs a line, camera, or LiDAR to correct itself.

Key concepts:

Accumulated error · systematic error · slippage · external reference.

🎯 PID control in practice

Why on/off control oscillates, the role of P, I, and D, a complete PID tested on a simulated motor, a method for tuning, anti-windup, and keeping two wheels at the same speed.

What it is:

The back-and-forth motion of a system that only knows “all or nothing,” and the idea of correcting in proportion to the error.

Why learn this:

This is the problem PID solves in cars, drones, 3D printers, and robot arms.

Key concepts:

Target · measurement · error · closed loop.

What it is:

Proportional steering, correcting for constant wind, and braking before you overshoot: the three letters explained with an analogy and three curves.

Why learn this:

Once you understand what each gain does, you can tune by reading the graph instead of guessing.

Key concepts:

Kp · Ki · Kd · overshoot · steady-state error.

What it is:

A complete PID at 100 Hz controlling a simulated motor, with gains adjustable through the Serial Monitor, running in Wokwi.

Why learn this:

This is the structure you can reuse for the line follower and every control system you build later.

Key concepts:

Fixed period · micros() · simulated plant · Serial Plotter.

What it is:

The P → I → D order for manual tuning, and the Ziegler-Nichols table based on the critical gain.

Why learn this:

A poorly tuned PID oscillates and heats up the motor; with a method, a few tests are enough.

Key concepts:

Ku · Tu · one gain at a time · test on the floor.

What it is:

The three pitfalls of real-world PID and the four lines of code that fix them.

Why learn this:

This is the difference between a PID that works in simulation and one that works on the robot.

Key concepts:

Saturation · anti-windup · derivative of the measurement · low-pass filter.

What it is:

One PI controller per wheel, the same RPM target, and each reading its own encoder: the rover drives straight.

Why learn this:

A stable foundation for the line follower and for ROS 2 /cmd_vel.

Key concepts:

One PID per wheel · measured RPM · PI is enough · reset the integral when stopping.

🧵 Several tasks at once

Why delay() stops the robot, non-blocking tasks with millis(), state machines, FreeRTOS tasks on both cores, queues and semaphores, and code organized into files and classes.

What it is:

What happens to sensors, PID, and communication while the program is paused in a delay().

Why learn this:

A robot that is “blind” for half a second hits an obstacle before it notices it.

Key concepts:

Blocking code · response time · main loop.

What it is:

Give each task its own clock: read a sensor every 20 ms, blink an LED every 500 ms, and send telemetry every 100 ms.

Why learn this:

This is the simplest way to do several things “at the same time” in a single loop.

Key concepts:

Periodic task · millis() rollover · cooperative scheduler.

What it is:

Draw the states, events, and transitions before writing the code, with entry and exit actions.

Why learn this:

Complex behavior without nested if statements; easy to test and change.

Key concepts:

State · event · transition · enum class.

What it is:

The real-time operating system that already runs inside the ESP32: create tasks with priorities and choose which core each one runs on.

Why learn this:

Motor control stays on schedule even while Wi-Fi and telemetry are using the processor.

Key concepts:

Task · priority · core · vTaskDelayUntil.

What it is:

How one task safely passes data to another without both changing the same variable at the same time.

Why learn this:

Prevents the hardest errors to track down: corrupted values that show up once an hour.

Key concepts:

Queue · mutex · semaphore · race condition.

What it is:

Separate motors, sensors, and control into .h/.cpp tabs and small classes within the sketch.

Why learn this:

The line follower from 2.6 and the robot from Track 3 can grow without turning into a thousand-line file.

Key concepts:

Class · header file (.h) · single responsibility · reusability.

📈 Telemetry and data logging

What to measure and how often, a live graph in the Serial Plotter, logging to a CSV file on the microSD card, MQTT over the network, a dashboard in a browser or in Node-RED/Grafana, and analysis in Python.

What it is:

Choose the variables that explain the robot’s behavior and the right rate for each one.

Why learn this:

Measuring everything overwhelms the processor; measuring too little doesn’t explain the failure.

Key concepts:

Sampling rate · timestamp · state variable.

What it is:

The "name:value" format that Arduino IDE 2 turns into curves, with several variables displayed together.

Why learn this:

It’s the fastest tool for tuning PID and finding noise.

Key concepts:

Label · scale · time window.

What it is:

Record data in a comma-separated text file, without a cable, while the robot moves for real.

Why learn this:

A robot far from the computer still needs to report what happened.

Key concepts:

SPI · CSV file · flush · FAT32 card.

What it is:

The lightweight protocol for publishing and subscribing to messages by topic, with a broker on the PC or Raspberry Pi.

Why learn this:

Multiple robots and dashboards communicate over the same channel; ROS 2 expands on this idea.

Key concepts:

Broker · topic · publish · subscribe.

What it is:

View telemetry in real time: a page served by the ESP32 itself or a dashboard built in Node-RED or Grafana.

Why learn this:

Seeing the robot "from the inside" helps you debug and present the project.

Key concepts:

Dashboard · time series · WebSocket · indicator.

What it is:

Open the CSV on your computer, calculate averages and errors, and plot before-and-after results.

Why learn this:

Use the numbers to decide whether the adjustment improved the robot, not just your impression.

Key concepts:

pandas · DataFrame · matplotlib · before-and-after comparison.

🏁 Project: PID line follower

Track and sensor bar, line error calculated with a weighted average, steering PID applied to the base speed, automatic calibration, telemetry-based tuning, and challenges against the clock.

What it is:

Build a track with black electrical tape and position the 5-sensor IR bar at the right height.

Why learn this:

Sensor height and spacing determine half of a line follower’s performance.

Key concepts:

TCRT5000 / QTR · sensor height · track contrast.

What it is:

Turn five readings into a single number: the line’s position relative to the center of the robot.

Why learn this:

A continuous error, rather than "left/right," is what makes a smooth PID possible.

Key concepts:

Weighted average · line position · line lost.

What it is:

Add the PID correction to one wheel and subtract it from the other, on top of a base speed.

Why learn this:

This is the architecture used by almost every competition line follower.

Key concepts:

Base speed · correction · per-wheel saturation.

What it is:

The robot turns over the line during the first few seconds and learns the minimum and maximum for each sensor.

Why learn this:

Ambient light and track conditions vary from place to place; calibrating on the spot saves you from rewriting the code.

Key concepts:

Minimum and maximum · normalization · EEPROM/Preferences.

What it is:

Record the line error for one lap, change one gain, record it again, and compare the graphs.

Why learn this:

Turn "it seems better" into "the average error dropped by half."

Key concepts:

Average error · lap time · one adjustment at a time.

What it is:

More difficult tracks, recognizing intersections and a lost line, and timing the lap.

Why learn this:

Prepares you for competitions such as OBR and for the vision-based robot in Track 3.

Key concepts:

Adaptive speed · last-error memory · timed lap.

📌 Track pin map

All of Track 2 uses the same map. It differs slightly from the Track 1 rover: the right motor moves from pins 33/32/4 to 14/16/17, and the driver’s STBY is connected directly to 3V3. This frees up ADC1 pins for the line sensors and pins 18/19 for the encoders or microSD.

✗ What can’t be connected at the same time

The encoders and microSD share pins 18 and 19, and the right encoder uses 34/35, which are also line sensor pins. So each project connects only what it uses: the PID project in 2.3 uses encoders without microSD; the line follower in 2.6 uses sensors and microSD without encoders.

🧰 Trail kit

Parts to add to the Track 1 rover. Approximate range of R$ 150 a R$ 350 for the extra set (Oct/2026; check before buying: prices vary by store, shipping, and exchange rate).

🧭 IMU

MPU-6050 module (GY-521), the cheapest and best-documented option. Alternative with a compass: ICM-20948 (9-axis), more expensive.

⚙️ Motors with encoders

Pair of N20 motors with magnetic encoders (two channels) and wheels, or the T1 TT motors with encoder disks and optical sensors.

〰️ Line sensors

Bar with 5 infrared sensors (TCRT5000) or a QTR bar, with analog output, for the line follower in 2.6.

💾 Logging

microSD card reader module (SPI, 3.3 V) and an 8 to 32 GB card formatted as FAT32.

🖥️ Optional

SSD1306 OLED display (I2C, 0.96") to show status, gains, and lap time on the robot itself.

🏁 Track

19 mm black electrical tape on white poster board, light MDF, or a smooth floor.

✓ Worth it

Two-channel encoders (they tell you the direction of rotation) and firm rubber wheels, which slip less and make odometry more reliable.

✗ Don’t buy these for this trail

Single-bit digital line sensors (they only say “line: yes/no”) and 5 V IMU modules without a regulator.
