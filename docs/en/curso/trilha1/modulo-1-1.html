🔁 What is a robot: sense, think, act

A robot is a machine that perceives its surroundings, decides what to do, and acts on the world, on its own and in a loop. It measures something (the distance to a wall), compares it with a goal (not to crash), and moves something (turns its wheels). Then it measures again, because the world has changed. This cycle repeats dozens or hundreds of times per second.

🌱 New here?

A sensor is the component that turns something in the world (light, distance, tilt) into an electrical signal the program can read. An actuator does the opposite: it receives a signal and produces movement, light, or sound (motor, servo, LED, buzzer). The controller is the “brain” that runs the program in between, like the ESP32 you’ll use in Track 1.

How to read this map: follow the green arrows from left to right (measure → decide → move), then follow the cyan arrow back. That return loop is what separates a robot from a remote-controlled toy: it checks the result of its own action.

✓ It’s a robot

  • ✓A vacuum that avoids the sofa on its own.
  • ✓A car that follows a black line on the floor.
  • ✓An arm that picks up the part where the camera found it.

✗ Not a robot (yet)

  • ✗Remote-controlled car: you make the decisions.
  • ✗Fan with a timer: it acts, but doesn’t sense anything.
  • ✗Digital thermometer: it senses, but doesn’t act.

Why learn this

Every project in this course, from the cardboard rover to the AI arm, uses the same cycle with different parts. When something doesn’t work, you’ll ask, “Is the problem with sensing, thinking, or acting?” and narrow down the fault before opening the code.

🌍 Robots in the real world in 2026

Most robots working today are unassuming: arms that weld cars, carts that carry boxes in warehouses, and vacuums that map rooms. What’s new in recent years is that affordable open-source arms, like the SO-101, and AI models that learn by demonstration have moved out of the lab and become accessible to people with a 3D printer. Humanoids show up a lot in videos, but they’re still expensive and work in controlled pilot programs.

How to read this map: the green dots are already at work every day in factories and homes. The cyan dots show where researchers and makers are working now. The humanoid’s dashed outline indicates that you’re more likely to see it in a demonstration than doing routine work.

🏭 Industrial robots and cobots

Arms that weld, paint, and assemble. The cobots (collaborative robots) work alongside people, with limited force and the ability to stop when touched.

🧹 Household robots

Vacuums and lawn mowers with LiDAR or a camera map the home and return to their charging base on their own.

📦 Logistics

The AMRs (autonomous mobile robots) carry shelves and boxes in warehouses, navigating with maps, as you’ll do in Track 4.

🦾 Open-source arms and AI

The SO-101 (printed parts and servos), paired with Hugging Face’s LeRobot, learns tasks by watching you demonstrate them. It’s at the heart of Track 5.

💡 Watch out for hype

A video of a robot making coffee may have been sped up, controlled by someone off camera, or repeated until it worked. Before you’re impressed, look for three things: actual speed (1x), stated autonomy (no one controlling it) and success rate (how many times it worked out of ten).

Why learn this

Knowing where robotics really stands helps you choose projects that work and avoid spending money on promises. The same building blocks you’ll put together (distance sensor, motor with encoder, camera with object detector, map) are inside the vacuum and warehouse robot.

🧠 Microcontroller vs. computer: ESP32, Pi 5, or Jetson?

A robot’s “brain” can be one of two types. A microcontroller, such as the ESP32, is a small chip that runs a single program as soon as it gets power: it starts up in less than a second, uses little battery power, and responds to sensors at the right time. A single-board computer, such as the Raspberry Pi 5, runs a full operating system (Linux), with a camera, network, Python, and AI, but takes longer to start and uses more power.

🌱 New here?

GPIO are the board’s general-purpose pins: each one can turn on an LED or read a button. Real time means responding within a guaranteed time limit, such as stopping the motor at exactly the right millisecond. TOPS (trillions of operations per second) measures how many AI calculations an accelerator can perform; the higher the number, the faster it runs vision models.

How to read this map: Start at the top and follow the question that describes your robot. The cyan line below reminds you that this isn’t an either-or choice: in robots on Learning Paths 3 to 5, the Raspberry Pi (or Jetson) does the thinking, while an ESP32 continues to handle the motors and emergency stop.

Choosing the wrong board can be costly: a Raspberry Pi used to blink an LED wastes battery; an ESP32 alone can’t run a modern object detector. Follow the rule “the computer thinks, the microcontroller controls” to build robots that can grow without rebuilding everything.

🧰 The starter kit, piece by piece

The robot in Learning Path 1 is a two-wheel rover that moves on its own and avoids obstacles. It uses just a few parts, all easy to find in Brazil, and each one corresponds to a part of the sense-think-act cycle. Buy them gradually: modules 1.1 to 1.3 work with just the simulator, or with the ESP32 and a few LEDs.

How to read this map: The front of the robot is at the top of the figure. Cyan = sense (ultrasonic and infrared), green = think and command (ESP32 and driver), gray = act (motors and wheels), amber = power. Every part you buy falls into one of these four colors.

✓ Buy this

  • ✓TB6612FNG or DRV8833 driver: efficient and compatible with the ESP32’s 3.3 V logic.
  • ✓Rechargeable NiMH batteries or 18650 cells with a protection board (BMS).
  • ✓USB-C cable that transfers data, not just power.
  • ✓Continuous-rotation servos, if you’d prefer them to TT motors.

✗ Avoid this

  • ✗Square 9 V battery for the motor: it delivers little current and runs out in minutes.
  • ✗L298N driver: still sold, but it loses about 2 V as heat.
  • ✗“37 sensors” kits: most of them end up in a drawer.
  • ✗Standard servo (180°) as a wheel: it doesn’t rotate continuously.

⚠️ Charging-only cable

The most common mistake in the first lesson: the board lights up, but the computer doesn’t recognize it. In nine out of ten cases, the USB cable only supplies power. Change the cable before reinstalling any software.

Why learn this

Knowing what each part is for avoids two frustrations: spending money on components you won't use and building a robot that won't move because the power supply or driver can't handle the motors. The kit above covers all of Track 1 and much of Track 2.

💻 Start without hardware: Wokwi and workbench safety

O Wokwi (wokwi.com) is a free browser-based simulator: you build a virtual ESP32, connect LEDs, sensors, and servos, paste in the code, and press play. It runs the same program that would run on the real board. It's the fastest way to make your first "blinking LED" today, before your kit arrives.

🌱 New here?

A sketch is the Arduino-style program: the function setup() runs once when the board powers on, and loop() repeats forever. The Serial Monitor is the window where the board writes text messages for you. The resistor limits the current so the LED doesn't burn out; the LED has a correct orientation: the long leg (anode) goes toward the pin, and the short leg (cathode) goes to GND, which is the negative terminal.

How to read this map: follow the green wire from GPIO 2 to the resistor (amber zigzag) and the LED (red triangle, with its point facing the short leg). The cyan wire completes the circuit at GND. Without a return path, nothing lights up.

Step by step in Wokwi

1

Create the project

At wokwi.com, click "ESP32" and choose the Arduino model (the C++ sketch). You don't need an account to try it.

2

Build the circuit

Click the "+" button to add an LED and a resistor. Connect them as shown in the figure: GPIO 2 → resistor → LED's long leg; short leg → GND. Click the resistor and set it to 220 Ω.

3

Paste in the code and press play

Delete the contents of sketch.ino, paste in the block below, and click the green button to start the simulation.

Goal: make the LED blink once per second and count the blinks in the Serial Monitor. It works the same in Wokwi and on a real ESP32 (the "esp32" 3.x package in Arduino IDE 2.x).

pisca-led.ino
// Blink LED: first program in the course (ESP32, esp32 3.x package)
// Turns on the GPIO 2 LED for half a second, then turns it off for half a second
// and counts the blinks in the Serial Monitor.

const int PINO_LED = 2;      // GPIO 2: external LED (and the blue LED on many DevKits)
const int TEMPO_MS = 500;    // half a second on, half a second off
unsigned long piscadas = 0;

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);         // Serial Monitor speed
  pinMode(PINO_LED, OUTPUT);    // the pin will send a signal (output)
  Serial.println("INEMA Robotics: LED blink ready");
}

void loop() {
  digitalWrite(PINO_LED, HIGH); // on: 3.3 V at the pin
  delay(TEMPO_MS);
  digitalWrite(PINO_LED, LOW);  // off: 0 V at the pin
  delay(TEMPO_MS);
  piscadas++;
  Serial.print("Blinks: ");
  Serial.println(piscadas);
}

How to check

  • ✓The LED turns on and off once per second.
  • ✓The Serial Monitor shows "INEMA Robotics: LED blink ready" and then "Blinks: 1", "Blinks: 2"...
  • ✗LED is off while the counter keeps going? The LED is reversed: swap its legs.

Challenge (PWM API 3.x): instead of blinking, make the LED "breathe" by gradually increasing and decreasing its brightness. In version 3.x, ledcAttach(pin, frequency, bits) sets up the pin, and ledcWrite(pin, value) takes the pin itself. If an older tutorial uses PWM functions with a separate "channel," it's for API 2.x and no longer compiles.

led-respira.ino
// LED that "breathes" using PWM (ESP32, esp32 3.x package)
const int PINO_LED = 2;

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  ledcAttach(PINO_LED, 5000, 8);   // 5 kHz, 8 bits: brightness from 0 to 255
  Serial.println("PWM ready on GPIO 2");
}

void loop() {
  for (int v = 0; v <= 255; v += 5) { ledcWrite(PINO_LED, v); delay(15); }
  for (int v = 255; v >= 0; v -= 5) { ledcWrite(PINO_LED, v); delay(15); }
}

How to check: the LED fades on and off smoothly, without abrupt blinking, about once every 1.5 seconds; the Serial Monitor shows "PWM ready on GPIO 2".

Workbench safety (from day one)

✓ Do

  • ✓Disconnect the battery before touching any wires.
  • ✓Test motors with the robot raised and its wheels off the ground.
  • ✓Connect all GND pins together (ESP32, driver, and battery).
  • ✓Check the polarity (+ and −) twice before powering up.

✗ Don't

  • ✗Power a motor from the ESP32's 3V3 pin: it can't handle the current.
  • ✗Connect 5 V to an ESP32 input pin (its logic voltage is 3.3 V).
  • ✗Let the battery's two terminals touch: a short circuit gets hot and can catch fire.
  • ✗Leave a lithium battery charging unattended.

💡 A teaching tip

Save your Wokwi project with a free account and keep the link. In Module 1.3, you'll open this same sketch in Arduino IDE 2.x and upload it to the real board; comparing the two shows that the simulator and the hardware run the same code.

Why learn this

Simulating first takes away the fear and cost of mistakes: you learn about pins, resistors, and code without risking damage. The safety habits you build with an LED are the same ones that will protect you when you're working with motors, lithium batteries, and a moving robotic arm.
